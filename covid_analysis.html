1 Study Data

This study uses a 10,000-record sample of reported COVID-19 cases restricted to the early pre-vaccine study period from March 15 through December 10, 2020.

Data were downloaded from the CDC COVID-19 Case Surveillance Public Use Data through the CDC Socrata API.

A reproducible computational subsample was constructed by randomly selecting 20 calendar dates within the study period using a fixed random seed (42). For each selected date, up to 500 records were retrieved from a contiguous block of surveillance records, with the starting position selected reproducibly using a date-specific random seed. Records were ordered by their internal Socrata identifier before retrieval.

The resulting dataset contains 10,000 surveillance records and 12 variables. Since sampling was performed by date and contiguous record blocks rather than by simple random sampling across all eligible cases, the sample should not be considered representative of all reported COVID-19 cases during the study period.

    "Data/covid_early_pandemic_sample_10k.csv",
    stringsAsFactors = FALSE

3.2 Risk Ratio and Risk Difference

Here \(a\) and \(b\) represent hospitalized and non-hospitalized exposed cases, respectively. Similarly, \(c\) and \(d\) represent hospitalized and non-hospitalized unexposed cases, respectively. The crude risk ratio (RR) and risk difference (RD) are defined as:

\[RR=\frac{a/(a+b)}{c/(c+d)} \qquad RD=\frac{a}{a+b}-\frac{c}{c+d}\]

Hospitalization risk is estimated separately among exposed and unexposed cases.

Code 
# Extract cells from exposure-outcome table
a <- exposure_outcome["Yes", "Yes"]
b <- exposure_outcome["Yes", "No"]
c <- exposure_outcome["No", "Yes"]
d <- exposure_outcome["No", "No"]

# Hospitalization risks
risk_exposed <- a / (a + b)
risk_unexposed <- c / (c + d)

# Effect measures
risk_ratio <- risk_exposed / risk_unexposed
risk_difference <- risk_exposed - risk_unexposed

# Display hospitalization risks and effect measures
round(c(
    Risk_Exposed = risk_exposed,
    Risk_Unexposed = risk_unexposed,
    Risk_Ratio = risk_ratio,
    Risk_Difference = risk_difference
), 3)
   Risk_Exposed  Risk_Unexposed      Risk_Ratio Risk_Difference 
          0.353           0.036           9.860           0.317

Among cases in the complete-case analytic cohort, hospitalization was reported for 35.3% of individuals with a reported pre-existing medical condition and 3.6% of individuals without one. The crude risk ratio was 9.86, indicating that the proportion hospitalized was approximately 9.86 times as high among individuals with reported medical conditions. The crude risk difference was 31.7 percentage points, representing the absolute difference in hospitalization proportions between the two groups.

3.3 95% Confidence Interval for Risk Ratio

A 95% confidence interval (CI) for the crude risk ratio was calculated using hte normal approximation to the log-tranformed risk ratio. The standard error of the log risk ratio is estimated as:

\[ SE[\ln(RR)] = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{a} - \frac{1}{a+b} + \frac{1}{c} - \frac{1}{c+d} } \]

The confidence interval is calculated on the logarithmic scale and then exponentiated to return to the risk-ratio scale.

\[ 95\% CI_{RR} = \exp\left[ \ln(RR) \pm 1.96 \times SE[\ln(RR)] \right] \]

Code 
# Standard error of the log risk ratio
se_log_rr <- sqrt(
    (1 / a) - (1 / (a + b)) +
    (1 / c) - (1 / (c + d))
)

# Calculate confidence limits on log scale
log_rr <- log(risk_ratio)

log_rr_lower <- log_rr - (1.96 * se_log_rr)
log_rr_upper <- log_rr + (1.96 * se_log_rr)

# Transform confidence limits back to risk-ratio scale
rr_lower <- exp(log_rr_lower)
rr_upper <- exp(log_rr_upper)

round(c(
    RR = risk_ratio,
    Lower_95_CI = rr_lower,
    Upper_95_CI = rr_upper
), 3)
         RR Lower_95_CI Upper_95_CI 
      9.860       6.719      14.470

The crude risk ratio was 9.86 (95% CI: 6.72–14.47). The confidence interval excludes the null value of 1, indicating evidence of a positive crude association between reported pre-existing medical condition and hospitalization.

3.4 95% Confidence Interval for Risk Difference

A 95% confidence interval for the crude risk difference was calculated using the normal approximation for the difference between two independent proportions. The standard error is estimated as:

\[ SE(RD) = \sqrt{ \frac{R_1(1-R_1)}{n_1} + \frac{R_0(1-R_0)}{n_0} } \]

Here, \(R_1\) and \(R_0\) represent the observed hospitalization proportions among exposed and unexposed cases, respectively, while \(n_1\) and \(n_0\) represent the corresponding group sizes.

The confidence interval is calculated directly on the risk-difference scale:

\[ 95\% CI_{RD} = RD \pm 1.96 \times SE(RD) \]

Code 
# Group sample sizes
n_exposed <- a + b
n_unexposed <- c + d

# Standard error of risk difference
se_rd <- sqrt(
    (risk_exposed * (1 - risk_exposed) / n_exposed) +
    (risk_unexposed * (1 - risk_unexposed) / n_unexposed)
)

# Calculate confidence limits
rd_lower <- risk_difference - (1.96 * se_rd)
rd_upper <- risk_difference + (1.96 * se_rd)

round(c(
    RD = risk_difference,
    Lower_95_CI = rd_lower,
    Upper_95_CI = rd_upper
), 3)
         RD Lower_95_CI Upper_95_CI 
      0.317       0.279       0.354

The crude risk difference was 31.7 percentage points (95% CI: 27.9–35.4 percentage points). The confidence interval excludes the null value of 0, indicating evidence of a positive crude difference in hospitalization proportions between reported pre-existing medical condition and hospitalization.

These estimates describe crude, unadjusted associations and do not account for potential confounding by age or sex. Both confidence intervals were calculated using the normal approximation, which may be inaccurate for small sample sizes or extreme proportions.

4.3 Summary of Potential Confounders

The descriptive comparisons were used to examine the relationships of age and sex with both reported pre-existing medical conditions and hospitalization. Differences in these distributions may contribute to the observed crude association.

Age and sex were retained as adjustment variables based on the hypothesized causal structure and prior literature review rather than solely on the descriptive results. The following adjusted analysis examines the exposure-outcome association while accounting for these variables.

5 Adjusted Analysis

Age and sex were identified as potential confounders based on the hypothesized causal structure and prior literature review. To examine the association between reported pre-existing medical conditions and hospitalization while accounting for these variables, a multivariable logistic regression model was fitted.

This indicates that the estimated odds of hospitalization were higher among cases with reported pre-existing medical conditions than among cases without, after accounting for age and sex.

5.2.1 Interpretation of Logistic Regression

Logistic regression models the log odds of a binary outcome as a function of the exposure and adjustment varialbes. The fitted model can be written as:

\[ \beta_0 + \beta_E E_i + \sum_{k=1}^{8}\beta_{A_k}A_{ik} + \beta_S S_i, \]

where

\[ p_i = P(Y_i = 1 \mid E_i, A_i, S_i). \]

Here, \(E_i\) represents reported pre-existing medical condition status, \(A_{ik}\) represents the eight indicator variables from the non-reference age groups, and \(S_i\) represents sex.

The exposure coefficient \(\beta_E\) represents the difference in conditional log odds of hospitalization between cases with and without reported pre-existing medical conditions, holding age group and sex constant.

Exponentiating this coefficient gives the adjusted odds ratio:

\[ OR_{\text{adjusted}} = e^{\beta_E}, \]

This odds ratio compares hospitalization odds between exposure groups at the same values of age and sex. The model assumes that the exposure odds ratio is constant across age and sex categories

5.3 Adjusted Odds Ratio

A 95% Wald confidence interval was calculated on the log-odds scale using the estimated coefficient and its standard error, then exponentiated to obtain confidence limits on the odds-ratio scale.

\[ OR_{\text{adjusted}} = e^{\hat{\beta}_E} \]

\[ 95\% CI_{OR} = \exp( \hat{\beta}_E \pm 1.96 \times SE(\hat{\beta}_E) ) \]

Code 
# Extract the exposure coefficient (log odds ratio)
beta <- coef(logistic_model)["medcondYes"]

# Exponentiate to obtain the adjusted odds ratio
adjusted_or <- exp(beta)

# Extract the coefficient variance and calculate its standard error
se <- sqrt(vcov(logistic_model)["medcondYes", "medcondYes"])

# Calculate 95% Wald confidence interval on the odds-ratio scale
or_ci <- exp(beta + c(-1, 1) * 1.96 * se)

# Display adjusted odds ratio and confidence interval
round(
    c(
        Adjusted_OR = adjusted_or,
        Lower_95_CI = or_ci[1],
        Upper_95_CI = or_ci[2]
    ),
    3
)
Adjusted_OR.medcondYes            Lower_95_CI            Upper_95_CI 
                 6.513                  4.134                 10.260

The fitted model estimate an adjusted odds ratio of 6.51 (95% CI: 4.13–10.26) for reported pre-existing medical conditions, conditional on age group and sex. This indicates that the estimated odds of hospitalization were higher among cases with reported pre-existing medical conditions than among cases without reported conditions, after accounting for age and sex.

5.4 Regression Standardization

Regression standardization was used to estimate hospitalization proportions for hte two exposure conditions while accounting for the age and sex distribution of the adjusted-analysis cohort.

This approach uses the fitted logistic regression model to predict hospitalization probabilities under two hypothetical exposure scenarios: one in which every case is assigned reported pre-existing medical conditions and another in which every case is assigned no reported pre-existing medical conditions.

Age and sex remain unchanged in both scenarios. This means the predicted probabilities are averages over the same obeserved distribution of age and sex, standardizing for these potential confounders.

5.4.1 Constructing Exposure Scenarios

Two copies of the adjusted-analysis cohort were created. Pre-existing medical condition status was set to Yes for every case in one copy and No for every case in the other copy. Age and sex were left unchanged in both copies.

Code

5.5 Adjusted Risk Ratio and Risk Difference

The standardized hospitalization proportions were used to calculate an adjusted risk ratio and risk difference.

The adjusted risk ratio compares the two standardized proportions:

\[ RR_{\text{adjusted}} = \frac{\bar{p}{E=1}}{\bar{p}{E=0}} \]

The adjusted risk difference represents their absolute difference:

\[ RD_{\text{adjusted}} = \bar{p}{E=1} - \bar{p}{E=0} \]

Where \(\bar{p}{E=1}\) and \(\bar{p}{E=0}\) are the mean model-predicted hospitalizaiton probabilities under the exposed and unexposed scenarios, respectively.

## Risk Ratio and Risk Difference

## 95% Confidence Interval for Risk Ratio

A 95% confidence interval (CI) for the crude risk ratio was calculated using hte normal approximation to the log-tranformed risk ratio. The standard error of the log risk ratio is estimated as:

$$
SE[\ln(RR)] =
\sqrt{
    \frac{1}{a}
    - \frac{1}{a+b}
    + \frac{1}{c}
    - \frac{1}{c+d}
}
$$

The confidence interval is calculated on the logarithmic scale and then exponentiated to return to the risk-ratio scale.

$$
95\% CI_{RR} =
\exp\left[
    \ln(RR) \pm 1.96 \times SE[\ln(RR)]
\right]
$$

```{r}
# Standard error of the log risk ratio
se_log_rr <- sqrt(
    (1 / a) - (1 / (a + b)) +
    (1 / c) - (1 / (c + d))
)

# Calculate confidence limits on log scale
log_rr <- log(risk_ratio)

log_rr_lower <- log_rr - (1.96 * se_log_rr)
log_rr_upper <- log_rr + (1.96 * se_log_rr)

# Transform confidence limits back to risk-ratio scale
rr_lower <- exp(log_rr_lower)
rr_upper <- exp(log_rr_upper)

round(c(
    RR = risk_ratio,
    Lower_95_CI = rr_lower,
    Upper_95_CI = rr_upper
), 3)
```

The crude risk ratio was `r round(risk_ratio, 2)` (95% CI: `r round(rr_lower, 2)`--`r round(rr_upper, 2)`). The confidence interval excludes the null value of 1, indicating evidence of a positive crude association between reported pre-existing medical condition and hospitalization.

## 95% Confidence Interval for Risk Difference

A 95% confidence interval for the crude risk difference was calculated using the normal approximation for the difference between two independent proportions. The standard error is estimated as:

$$
SE(RD) =
\sqrt{
    \frac{R_1(1-R_1)}{n_1}
    + \frac{R_0(1-R_0)}{n_0}
}
$$

Here, $R_1$ and $R_0$ represent the observed hospitalization proportions among exposed and unexposed cases, respectively, while $n_1$ and $n_0$ represent the corresponding group sizes.

The confidence interval is calculated directly on the risk-difference scale:

$$
95\% CI_{RD} = RD \pm 1.96 \times SE(RD)
$$

```{r}
# Group sample sizes
n_exposed <- a + b
n_unexposed <- c + d

# Standard error of risk difference
se_rd <- sqrt(
    (risk_exposed * (1 - risk_exposed) / n_exposed) +
    (risk_unexposed * (1 - risk_unexposed) / n_unexposed)
)

# Calculate confidence limits
rd_lower <- risk_difference - (1.96 * se_rd)
rd_upper <- risk_difference + (1.96 * se_rd)

round(c(
    RD = risk_difference,
    Lower_95_CI = rd_lower,
    Upper_95_CI = rd_upper
), 3)
```

The crude risk difference was `r round(risk_difference * 100, 1)` percentage points (95% CI: `r round(rd_lower * 100, 1)`--`r round(rd_upper * 100, 1)` percentage points). The confidence interval excludes the null value of 0, indicating evidence of a positive crude difference in hospitalization proportions between reported pre-existing medical condition and hospitalization.

These estimates describe crude, unadjusted associations and do not account for potential confounding by age or sex. Both confidence intervals were calculated using the normal approximation, which may be inaccurate for small sample sizes or extreme proportions.

```{r}
    "Std. Error"
]

# Calculate 95% confidence limits on the log-odds scale
or_log_lower <- exposure_beta - 1.96 * exposure_se
or_log_upper <- exposure_beta + 1.96 * exposure_se

# Transform confidence limits to the odds-ratio scale
or_lower <- exp(or_log_lower)
or_upper <- exp(or_log_upper)

# Display adjusted odds ratio and confidence interval
round(
    c(
        Adjusted_OR = adjusted_or,
        Lower_95_CI = or_lower,
        Upper_95_CI = or_upper
    ),
    3
)
```

The fitted model estimated an adjusted odds ratio of `r round(adjusted_or, 2)` (95% CI: `r round(or_lower, 2)`--`r round(or_upper, 2)`) for reported pre-existing medical conditions, controlling for age group and sex. This indicates that the estimated odds of hospitalization were higher among cases with reported pre-existing medical conditions than among cases without, after accounting for age and sex.

### Interpretation of Logistic Regression

Logistic regression models the log odds of a binary outcome as a function of the exposure and adjustment varialbes. The fitted model can be written as:

$$
\beta_0 + \beta_E E_i + \sum_{k=1}^{8}\beta_{A_k}A_{ik} + \beta_S S_i,
$$

where

$$
p_i = P(Y_i = 1 \mid E_i, A_i, S_i).
$$

Here, $E_i$ represents reported pre-existing medical condition status, $A_{ik}$ represents the eight indicator variables from the non-reference age groups, and $S_i$ represents sex.

The exposure coefficient $\beta_E$ represents the difference in conditional log odds of hospitalization between cases with and without reported pre-existing medical conditions, holding age group and sex constant.

Exponentiating this coefficient gives the adjusted odds ratio:

$$
OR_{\text{adjusted}} = e^{\beta_E},
$$

This odds ratio compares hospitalization odds between exposure groups at the same values of age and sex. The model assumes that the exposure odds ratio is constant across age and sex categories

## Adjusted Odds Ratio

A 95% Wald confidence interval was calculated on the log-odds scale using the estimated coefficient and its standard error, then exponentiated to obtain confidence limits on the odds-ratio scale.

$$
OR_{\text{adjusted}} = e^{\hat{\beta}_E}
$$

$$
95\% CI_{OR} =
\exp(
    \hat{\beta}_E \pm 1.96 \times SE(\hat{\beta}_E)
)
$$

```{r}
# Extract the exposure coefficient (log odds ratio)
beta <- coef(logistic_model)["medcondYes"]

# Exponentiate to obtain the adjusted odds ratio
adjusted_or <- exp(beta)

# Extract the coefficient variance and calculate its standard error
se <- sqrt(vcov(logistic_model)["medcondYes", "medcondYes"])

# Calculate 95% Wald confidence interval on the odds-ratio scale
or_ci <- exp(beta + c(-1, 1) * 1.96 * se)

# Display adjusted odds ratio and confidence interval
round(
    c(
        Adjusted_OR = adjusted_or,
        Lower_95_CI = or_ci[1],
        Upper_95_CI = or_ci[2]
    ),
    3
)
```

The fitted model estimate an adjusted odds ratio of `r round(adjusted_or, 2)` (95% CI: `r round(or_ci[1], 2)`--`r round(or_ci[2], 2)`) for reported pre-existing medical conditions, conditional on age group and sex. This indicates that the estimated odds of hospitalization were higher among cases with reported pre-existing medical conditions than among cases without reported conditions, after accounting for age and sex.

## Regression Standardization

Regression standardization was used to estimate hospitalization proportions for hte two exposure conditions while accounting for the age and sex distribution of the adjusted-analysis cohort.

This approach uses the fitted logistic regression model to predict hospitalization probabilities under two hypothetical exposure scenarios: one in which every case is assigned reported pre-existing medical conditions and another in which every case is assigned no reported pre-existing medical conditions.

Age and sex remain unchanged in both scenarios. This means the predicted probabilities are averages over the same obeserved distribution of age and sex, standardizing for these potential confounders.

### Constructing Exposure Scenarios

Two copies of the adjusted-analysis cohort were created. Pre-existing medical condition status was set to `Yes` for every case in one copy and `No` for every case in the other copy. Age and sex were left unchanged in both copies.

```{r}
# Create two copies of the adjusted cohort
everyone_exposed <- adjusted_analysis
everyone_unexposed <- adjusted_analysis

# Assign reported medical conditions to every case
everyone_exposed$medcond <- factor(
    "Yes",
    levels = levels(adjusted_analysis$medcond)
)

# Assign no reported medical conditions to every case
everyone_unexposed$medcond <- factor(
    "No",
    levels = levels(adjusted_analysis$medcond)
)
```

These scenarios are used to generate model-based predictions. They do not represent actual observed data, but rather hypothetical situations in which every case is assigned the same exposure status while retaining their observed age and sex.

### Predicting Standardized Hospitalization Proportions

The fitted logistic regression model was used to predict hospitalization probabilities for each case under both exposure scenarios.

*Setting `type = "response"` returns the predicted probabilities rather than predicted log odds*

```{r}
# Predict hospitalization probabilities under each exposure scenario
pred_exposed <- predict(
    logistic_model,
    newdata = everyone_exposed,
    type = "response"
)

pred_unexposed <- predict(
    logistic_model,
    newdata = everyone_unexposed,
    type = "response"
)

# Average predicted probabilities across the adjusted cohort
adjusted_risk_exposed <- mean(pred_exposed)
adjusted_risk_unexposed <- mean(pred_unexposed)

# Display standardized hospitalization proportions
round(
    c(
        Exposed = adjusted_risk_exposed,
        Unexposed = adjusted_risk_unexposed
    ),
    4
)
```

The standardized hospitalization proportion under reported pre-existing medical conditions was `r round(adjusted_risk_exposed * 100, 1)`%, compared with `r round(adjusted_risk_unexposed * 100, 1)`% under no reported medical conditions.

These are model-based estimates averaged across the observed age and sex distribution of the adjusted-analysis cohort, rather than the directly observed hospitalization proportions within each exposure group.

## Adjusted Risk Ratio and Risk Difference

The standardized hospitalization proportions were used to calculate an adjusted risk ratio and risk difference.

The adjusted risk ratio compares the two standardized proportions:

$$
RR_{\text{adjusted}} =
\frac{\bar{p}{E=1}}{\bar{p}{E=0}}
$$

The adjusted risk difference represents their absolute difference:

$$
RD_{\text{adjusted}} =
\bar{p}{E=1} - \bar{p}{E=0}
$$

Where $\bar{p}{E=1}$ and $\bar{p}{E=0}$ are the mean model-predicted hospitalizaiton probabilities under the exposed and unexposed scenarios, respectively.

```{r}
# Calculate adjusted risk ratio and risk difference
adjusted_rr <- adjusted_risk_exposed / adjusted_risk_unexposed
adjusted_rd <- adjusted_risk_exposed - adjusted_risk_unexposed

# Display adjusted association measures
round(
    c(
        Adjusted_RR = adjusted_rr,
        Adjusted_RD = adjusted_rd,
        Adjusted_RD_Percentage_Points = adjusted_rd * 100
    ),
    3
)
```

The standardized adjusted risk ratio was `r round(adjusted_rr, 2)`, indicating that the model-estimated hospitalization proportion under reported pre-existing medical conditions was approximately `r round(adjusted_rr, 2)` times the proportion under no reported medical conditions, after standardizing to the same age and sex distribution.

The adjusted risk difference was `r round(adjusted_rd * 100, 1)` percentage points, representing the absolute difference between the two standardized hospitalization proportions.

These estimates describe model-adjusted associations within the analytic cohort. They do not establish a causal effect of pre-existing medical conditions on hospitalization. In particular, adjustment for age and sex does not eliminate potential residual confounding, selection bias associated with missing surveillance data, or limitations arising from the sampling procedure.
